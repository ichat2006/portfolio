TrueTake

A pipeline that decides which recorded robot-training clips a lab should accept. Its core is recovering the clock offset between a phone's video and its gyroscope, which also proves the footage is genuine.

The problem

Robotics labs pay people to record demonstrations, such as folding a shirt on video, as training data. Many clips turn out to be unusable: badly framed, idle, duplicated, or not genuine (a re-filmed screen, a borrowed sensor file, a spliced segment). TrueTake is a pipeline that decides which clips to accept and explains every decision with timestamped reasons and usable minutes per clip. It is built as a cheap-first cascade. Fast deterministic checks decide most clips, and a vision-language model (Claude) is called only for the cases those checks can't settle.

  • Layer 1, quality: resolution, frame rate, orientation, blur, idle time, and whether the task is in frame. Cheap CLIP checks run first; Claude judges only the uncertain middle and has to cite frames as evidence.
  • Layer 2, verification: does the video's motion match the phone's own gyroscope? (the main section below)
  • Layer 3, value: how much new behavior a clip adds compared with the clips already kept.

Verification is a clock-synchronization problem

A phone records video and motion sensors at the same time, but into two different files, on two different clocks, at two different rates. If the footage is real and unedited, the way the picture rotates must match the way the phone rotated. Testing that means first answering a harder question: what is the time offset between the two files? Labs need that offset anyway to align video with sensor data, so one computation does double duty. It gives a match score (is it genuine?) and a clock offset (how to sync it).

Step by step

1. Camera rotation from pixels. For each pair of consecutive frames: grayscale, downscale to 320 px wide, pick about 200 corners (goodFeaturesToTrack), track them into the next frame with pyramidal Lucas–Kanade optical flow, and fit a rotation-plus-translation (partial affine) model with RANSAC so hands and moving cloth are rejected as outliers. That yields horizontal shift dx, vertical shift dy, and in-image rotation dθ. Dividing by the inter-frame time gives three rate signals: pan, tilt, and roll.

2. Put the gyro on the video's time grid, at the right instant. A finite difference between frames k and k+1 estimates the rate at their midpoint, not at either frame. So each gyro axis is interpolated onto frame-pair midpoints. Sampling at frame times instead would bake in a systematic half-frame error (about 17 ms at 30 fps) that the cross-correlation would then report as part of the clock offset.

3. Clean both signals. A 0.2 s moving average suppresses tracking jitter, and subtracting each signal's mean removes constant terms such as gyro bias or a slow steady pan.

4. Figure out which axis is which. How gyro axes map to pan, tilt, and roll depends on how the phone was held. For each video signal, the pipeline tries all three gyro axes with both signs (18 candidate pairings) and keeps the best-correlating one. The mapping is learned once per device and orientation on a known-good clip and then locked, so an attacker can't benefit from the pipeline searching harder on a bad clip.

5. Cross-correlate over lag. For each matched pair, compute the normalized cross-correlation at every lag from −2 s to +2 s:

r(τ) = Σₜ (vₜ − v̄)(gₜ₊τ − ḡ)  /  ( √Σₜ(vₜ − v̄)² · √Σₜ(gₜ₊τ − ḡ)² )

The peak height is the match score; the lag at the peak is the clock offset. Because Pearson correlation ignores scale, you never need the camera's focal length. For small rotations, image shift is about focal length × angular rate × Δt, so the video signal is a scaled copy of the gyro signal, and the unknown scale drops out.

6. Make the three axes vote. Pan, tilt, and roll must all peak at nearly the same lag (within 2 frames). One axis can produce a spurious peak by chance; three independent axes agreeing on the same offset is much harder to fake.

7. Look inside the clip. The same correlation runs on 5-second windows sliding by 1 second. A window where the match collapses inside an otherwise matching clip marks a splice or an inserted segment, and the report gives its timestamps.

8. Decide without false accusations. If the gyro barely moved (its spread is below a small threshold), the result is "not enough motion to verify," not a failure, because a still camera proves nothing either way. Otherwise a clip passes when its score clears a threshold set on training clips (the level that keeps 95% of genuine clips), and offsets over 1 second are flagged.

What each attack looks like to the math

AttackSignature
Sensor file from a different clipLow peak at every lag
Sensor file with its clock shiftedStrong peak, but at the wrong offset
Screen re-filmed from a tripodVideo shows motion, gyro is flat: no match
Spliced or inserted segmentMatch collapses in specific windows
Stock or generated video with made-up sensor dataLow peak
Screen re-filmed with a hand-held phonePartial match because the phone really moved; a known gap I document rather than hide

Practical limits I designed around: phone video stabilization removes the very shake being matched, so it must be off; close-up hands and cloth add motion that isn't camera rotation, so thresholds come from real tabletop clips; heavy motion blur loses tracked corners, so those frames count as inconclusive. Tracking 200 points at 320 px is light enough that a 30-second clip takes seconds on a laptop.

Value: which clips actually teach a robot something new

Only clips that pass quality and verification are scored, so "unusual" can't just mean "broken." Each clip gets three views, each with its own distance:

  • Scene: CLIP ViT-B/32 embeddings of 8 frames, averaged; distance is 1 − cosine similarity.
  • Hand motion: MediaPipe wrist, thumb-tip, and index-tip tracks, normalized to frame size and resampled to 64 steps; distance is dynamic time warping.
  • Strategy: Claude labels the fold as a sequence from a fixed step list (smooth, sleeves-in, fold-half, fold-thirds, roll, drop, recover, and so on); distance is normalized edit distance.

Each view's distances are divided by that view's median and averaged. Ranking is greedy farthest-point selection: start from the most typical clip, then repeatedly add the clip farthest from everything already chosen. A clip's value is its distance to the nearest chosen clip when it's added, and a clip whose nearest neighbor is too close is marked redundant, with its twin named in the report.

Evaluation and how I built it

Impact. We ran TrueTake unattended overnight on each day's recordings. Across our runs it settled roughly 70% of clips on its own on average (accepted or rejected, each with timestamped reasons), so people only had to review the remaining ~30%, could do that review outside the lab, and started each day with already-screened data to test on.

Every check was scored against hand labels on held-out clips plus synthetic attacks generated for each attack type above, reporting attacks caught, genuine clips wrongly flagged, and inconclusive clips, compared with a random baseline. Per-clip model calls and frames processed are logged so cost and failure modes can be traced. I used Claude Code as a coding agent, one module per prompt, and reviewed and tested every generated file on real clips before committing it. The two bugs it is easiest to get wrong, mixed timestamp units (ns, ms, s) and degrees versus radians, are checked by printing duration and gyro range on load and by plotting both signals together in Rerun before trusting any score.

What I'd push further

  • Sub-frame clock offset by fitting a parabola through the correlation peak and its neighbors, instead of snapping to the nearest midpoint sample.
  • Rolling-shutter correction: each image row is captured at a slightly different time, which skews rotation estimates during fast motion.
  • Read gyro data embedded in action-camera files (GoPro and similar) so verification works beyond phones.
