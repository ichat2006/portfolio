The problem

Robotics labs collect first-person task videos, such as folding a shirt or washing dishes, as robot-training data. Many clips turn out to be unusable: the task isn't actually done, hands leave the frame, the camera is idle, a clip duplicates another, or the footage isn't what it claims to be. TrueTake reviews a batch against a lab's written requirements (a YAML contract) and gives every clip one of four outcomes, APPROVED, NEEDS REVIEW, REJECTED, or DUPLICATE, each with timestamped reasons and usable minutes.

  • Quality: format and privacy rules, hand presence, idle time, and whether the requested goal and process are visible. Measured rules are decided by formulas against the contract's targets and review bands.
  • Verification: does the camera motion in the video match the camera's own gyroscope? (the main section below)
  • Value: ranks approved clips by how much new behavior they add, flags duplicates, and suggests what to film next.
  • Keeping the AI honest: Claude must return evidence before any yes/no/unsure goal answer. A validator checks every cited frame ID, timestamp, and evidence reference; Claude never overrides a formula, and unsure or invalid evidence becomes NEEDS REVIEW rather than a silent pass.

Verification is a clock-synchronization problem

A head-mounted camera records video and gyroscope data into separate streams, on separate clocks, at different rates. If the footage is genuine and unedited, the way the picture rotates must match the way the camera rotated. Testing that first requires a harder answer: what is the time offset between the two streams? One computation gives both a match score (do they agree?) and a clock offset (how to align them), which labs need anyway to sync video with sensor data.

How it works, step by step

1. Camera rotation from pixels. For each pair of sampled frames: pick up to 200 corners (goodFeaturesToTrack), track them with pyramidal Lucas–Kanade optical flow (21 px window, 3 pyramid levels), and fit a rotation-plus-translation model with RANSAC (2 px reprojection error) so hands and moving objects are rejected as outliers. A pair is discarded if fewer than 20 points track, under half are inliers, or the frames are more than 0.2 s apart. The fit gives pan and tilt in pixels/s and roll in rad/s.

2. Time-stamp each measurement at the right instant. A difference between two frames measures the rate at their midpoint, so each flow sample is stored at the pair's actual midpoint time. Stamping it at either frame would build a half-interval error into the signal, which the cross-correlation would then misreport as clock offset.

3. Put both signals on one grid without inventing data. Video rates are resampled onto a 0.1 s grid and smoothed with a centered 0.2 s window. Gyro values are interpolated onto the same grid, shifted by each candidate lag. Interpolation refuses to bridge any gap longer than 0.15 s, including after shifting, so a dropped stretch of sensor data stays missing instead of being filled with a straight line that could fake agreement.

4. Search lag and axis mapping together. For every lag from −2 s to +2 s in 0.1 s steps (41 lags), the code computes the Pearson correlation between each video axis and each gyro axis, wherever at least 50 valid pairs cover at least 5 s and both signals actually move. Which gyro axis is pan, tilt, or roll depends on how the camera is mounted, so it searches every signed permutation of the three axes. The score is the mean signed correlation over at least two informative axes, and the best (lag, mapping) pair wins:

r(τ) = Σₜ (vₜ − v̄)(gₜ₊τ − ḡ)  /  ( √Σₜ(vₜ − v̄)² · √Σₜ(gₜ₊τ − ḡ)² )
score(τ, π, s) = mean over informative axes i of  sᵢ · rᵢ,π(ᵢ)(τ)

Because Pearson correlation ignores scale, the camera's focal length is never needed: for small rotations, image shift is roughly focal length × angular rate × Δt, so the video signal is a scaled copy of the gyro signal and the unknown scale drops out.

5. Calibrate once, then lock. An exploratory search can find a mapping that fits a bad clip by chance, so it never earns a pass. A calibrated profile, fitted only on training clips, fixes the signed mapping (for the GoPro setup in our data: pan↔gz, tilt↔gy, roll↔gx, all positive) and the acceptance threshold (0.29). Profiles are validated: a provisional profile can't carry a threshold, and a calibrated one must list the training clips it came from.

6. Make the axes agree. The two strongest axes must each peak at nearly the same lag (within 0.2 s). Only the two strongest vote, because camera stabilization can flatten one axis, and a weak axis shouldn't veto two strong, independent ones.

7. Refuse to guess. No verdict is issued when the gyro barely moved (standard deviation under 0.02 rad/s), when overlap is too short, when the best lag sits at the ±2 s search boundary (the true offset may be outside the window), or when the score falls inside a review band (a close call). Those clips go to NEEDS REVIEW. Offsets over 1 s are flagged.

8. Localize edits. The same measurement runs on 5-second windows starting every second. A window where agreement collapses inside an otherwise matching clip marks where a splice is. Windows are evidence for the reviewer; they don't decide the verdict, because overlapping windows aren't independent tests.

The gyro scores are calibration and development results on a small clip set, not held-out accuracy, and the project says so. Gyro agreement is supporting evidence, not proof of authenticity. Uncached processing runs roughly 2.5 minutes per clip on a laptop CPU.

Value: which clips actually teach a robot something new

Only approved clips are ranked, so "unusual" can't just mean "broken." Each pair of clips gets three distances:

  • Scene: averaged CLIP embeddings; distance is 1 − cosine similarity.
  • Hand motion: hand paths resampled to 64 steps; distance is dynamic time warping.
  • Strategy: Claude's labeled step sequence for the task; distance is normalized edit distance.

Each view is divided by its median and averaged. Ranking is greedy farthest-point selection: start from the most representative clip, then repeatedly add the one farthest from everything already chosen. Near-identical clips are caught separately by comparing perceptual hashes of 8 frames, including a mirrored copy, so a flipped re-upload still counts as a duplicate.

What I'd push further

  • A held-out test set with complete human labels, so accuracy can be reported instead of development scores.
  • Sub-sample clock offset by fitting a parabola through the correlation peak instead of snapping to the 0.1 s grid.
  • Rolling-shutter correction, since each image row is captured at a slightly different time during fast motion.
  • Automatic escalation to a stronger model for unsure goal answers, which is designed but not yet implemented.
